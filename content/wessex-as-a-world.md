In December 2025, I started reading Thomas Hardy's Wessex novels. He built the world across thirteen novels and forty years of writing. Wessex is a literary name for the southwest English counties where he lived. The same towns recur under different names, with the same roads connect the same places. But it functions as something more: across the full arc of novels, we read Wessex and its people yielding themselves (slowly, then rapidly) to modernity via industrialization.

The early novels are set before the railway arrives when tradition and proximity shape the villages. By the end, that world is unperserved. Each replacement of one arrangement by another feeling plausible in isolation, devastating in aggregate. A shifting of paradigm rarely begin with unanimity. With this in mind, a throughline I'm observing is the dissonance running across the novels, with its past pressing against the present.
